More Than Data: Making Knowledge Graphs Work Together for Actionable Insights

Prof. Dr. Ricardo Usbeck

Abstract

While large language models (LLMs) have driven remarkable progress in natural language processing, their application in high-stakes, dynamic environments remains constrained by limited factual grounding, interpretability, and user trust. To address these fundamental challenges, our research group (https://www.leuphana.de/aix) investigates the neuro-symbolic integration of state-of-the-art language models with a multitude of dynamic knowledge graphs. By anchoring generative architectures in structured, continuously updated real-world facts, we enable AI systems to predict supply chain reliability or the trajectories of natural disasters. This keynote will highlight our recent contributions to the deployment of domain-specific, autonomous AI agents for crisis management and supply chain resilience. Drawing on insights from the COYPU, PROVIDER, and RescueMate projects - where robust, real-time event extraction from heterogeneous global data streams is critical - this talk will show how only a collection of integrated Knowledge Graphs can provide the verifiable ground truth necessary to deploy autonomous AI in environments where failure is not an option.

From Paraphrase to Provenance: Building a Verbatim Claim-Spine from Spoken-Word Archives

Chad Nantais, Syntopic

Abstract

Most pipelines that turn unstructured text or audio into a knowledge graph quietly introduce a provenance gap. A language model is asked to extract "the claims," and it returns fluent paraphrases that no speaker ever actually said, often stamped with timestamps the model guessed rather than measured. The resulting graph reads well and cites nothing verifiable. This talk is a lessons-learned account of discovering that failure mode in a production pipeline and rearchitecting the extraction around a strict provenance contract.

The setting is Syntopic, a boutique service that transforms finished podcast back-catalogs into research-grade companion sites with synced transcripts, entity pages, and full-text search over a structured claim graph. Our worked example is an 81-episode philosophy archive (roughly 170 hours of audio) rendered into a navigable knowledge graph of claims, concepts, people, and works. Midway through the build we found our extraction was storing synthesized claim prose with LLM-estimated timestamps rather than verbatim spans anchored to deterministic, word-level speech-to-text output. That violated the core promise of the product, so we paused and separated every claim into a normalized headline plus one or more verbatim grounding spans, each carrying a derived timestamp from word-level Whisper output and an epistemic status tag (asserted, hedged, contested, or reported).

The talk covers the schema decisions this forced (stable entity IDs and canonical names kept separate from surface mentions, so the graph can survive re-processing and multi-client federation without migration), the human-in-the-loop correction log we capture as a compounding training asset, and the practical entity-resolution ceiling we hit as archives grow past a couple hundred episodes. The aim is to share concrete, reproducible tactics for anyone extracting grounded, citable claims from speech, where the difference between "sounds right" and "provably said" is the whole point.

A live companion site for a completed 81-episode archive will be available at presentation time.

Abstract

Product configuration models in CPQ systems are typically proprietary and opaque, creating vendor lock-in and limiting flexibility. Transforming a Database into a Conversational Knowledge Graph: Tales from the ScaleTrust Project

Felix Brei, Institute for Applied Informatics (InfAI)

Abstract

How can a traditional data marketplace evolve into a conversational Knowledge Graph that enables users to discover data without having to understand complex query languages or graph structures? This talk presents the evolution of the ScaleTrust project, following the transformation of a relational database into a Knowledge Graph and, ultimately, into a conversational interface powered by an LLM agent.

Starting from a data marketplace backed by a relational database, the project introduced an RDF-based Knowledge Graph to enable users to explore datasets through their relationships, properties, keywords, categories, and authors. The talk describes the resulting RDF pipeline, including JSON-to-RDF mapping with YARRRML, SHACL validation, storage and querying with Apache Jena/Fuseki, and versioning with QuitStore. While this graph-based approach enabled richer exploration, it still required users to find an initial entry point and interact with a rather technical interface.

The next step was therefore to introduce an LLM agent capable of conducting the initial search and interacting with the Knowledge Graph through dedicated tools such as keyword search and SPARQL queries. Based on a ReAct architecture, the agent combines the ScaleTrust Knowledge Graph with external knowledge sources, evaluates results through sub-agents, and can even support users in submitting requests when no suitable dataset is found.

The talk shares the technical approach, lessons learned, and practical experiences from implementing this transformation within roughly one year, demonstrating how Semantic Web technologies and LLMs can complement each other to make Knowledge Graph-based data discovery more accessible.

Semantica: A Context and Accountability Layer for Knowledge Graph-Powered AI System (online)

Mohammad Kaif, Semantica

Abstract

Knowledge Graphs have become an essential component of modern AI systems, yet most LLM-based applications still rely on vector stores and short-term memory mechanisms that provide limited explainability, provenance, and accountability. As AI systems move into production, particularly in regulated domains, there is a growing need for systems that can answer not only what was generated, but also why a decision was made and where the underlying evidence originated.

In this talk, we present Semantica, an open-source framework that introduces Context Graphs as a persistent intelligence layer for AI systems. Semantica combines knowledge graph construction, provenance tracking, deterministic reasoning, ontology management, and temporal intelligence in a single platform. The framework supports the complete lifecycle of knowledge graphs, from multi-source ingestion and information extraction to entity resolution, reasoning, governance, and interactive exploration.

We will demonstrate how Context Graphs extend traditional Knowledge Graphs by treating decisions, evidence, and provenance as first-class graph entities, enabling explainable AI, auditable decision histories, and richer GraphRAG workflows. We also discuss practical applications in regulatory intelligence, compliance, and enterprise AI systems, and explore opportunities for integrating provenance-aware and reasoning-centric capabilities into the broader DBpedia ecosystem.

Session details
Bridging Law and AI: Semantic Precision without Compromising Trust

Translating legislation into digital implementations is a labor-intensive process. It requires analyzing large volumes of legal text with a level of accuracy and reliability that typically demands a multidisciplinary team of both legal and IT experts. Currently, this work is largely done by hand, translating legislation into structured knowledge, creating legal knowledge graphs based on legal ontologies, where legal norms are translated into logical if-then rules. The initial focus is on identifying accurate candidate definitions for key legal concepts, including their precise knowledge references, given strict definition guidelines. Secondly, we build on our previous work (“A New Approach to Taxonomy Creation”) to generate candidate taxonomies with definitions written according to the same guidelines. In this tutorial we will share our results and give a live demo of what is currently possible. We look forward to active participation, discussing the most promising way forward and the challenges that arising when combining semantic and AI technology in the domain of legal analysis.

Classifying Classes in Wikidata

Ege Atacan Doğan, JMU Würzburg

Abstract

In Wikidata there are many classes but many of them do not have direct instances. For example, builder is a class in Wikidata, yet everyone who is a builder is typed only as human and has occupation builder. This is a representational choice in Wikidata. Wikidata has many instances of human, which are also instances of organism, but no human is directly marked as an instance of organism. There is no fundamental reason to not allow direct instances of organization, but in Wikidata human is the "appropriate" class for humans. The question then becomes: how can these choices be maintained at the scale of Wikidata, particularly with the many editors of Wikidata.

One distinction that helps is sortality. Human, tree, and astronomical object type are sortal concepts. These classes individuate and supply identity criteria. Jazz, red, and happiness are not sortal. There are no things that are instances of them. Wikidata offers no built-in way to mark this difference, but the distribution of direct instance assertions provides empirical signal.

Within sortal classes, a second distinction matters: not every sortal is the "right" sortal for typing. Organism is too generic to be informative, male builder is too specific and, perhaps more importantly, non-rigid. The Wikidata community has converged on a preferred level of granularity, and we can see this choice in the density of direct instance assertions.

We propose a six-way categorisation of entities with respect to direct instance usage: individuals, appropriate classes, too-vague classes, too-specific classes, appropriate classes with no typed instances, and non-sortal classes that stand outside this dynamic entirely. We use intensional assumptions that are supported by what the classes "mean" to distinguish these categories. The result can be used to detect class-ordering problems and formalise the "appropriate class" modeling pattern.

An Explainable Hybrid GraphRAG Framework for Diabetes Risk Stratification (online)

Herman Kandolo, Vietnam National University & La Rochelle Université

Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) have significantly improved the usability of clinical decision support systems, but they still suffer from high context consumption and limited explainability when reasoning over medical data. We present an explainable Hybrid GraphRAG framework that shifts statistical reasoning from the LLM to a deterministic knowledge graph, allowing the language model to focus exclusively on generating clinician-friendly explanations. The framework combines unsupervised learning, supervised classification, Neo4j-based knowledge graphs, and LLM-based natural language generation into a unified decision-support pipeline.

The system builds epidemiological knowledge from the BRFSS 2015 dataset through K-Means clustering and stores the resulting profiles in a Neo4j knowledge graph. During online inference, a new patient is assigned to the most appropriate epidemiological profile using a K-NN classifier. The corresponding statistical evidence is retrieved through Cypher queries and provided to the LLM as a compact and fully auditable context for explanation generation. Experimental results demonstrate a 97.31% reduction in LLM context size compared with a vector-based RAG baseline while eliminating numerical hallucinations in our evaluation and providing fully traceable decision evidence. The presentation will demonstrate the complete workflow of the prototype and discuss how deterministic knowledge graphs can improve transparency and trust in GraphRAG-based clinical decision support systems.

DBpedia KG Catalog

Milan Dojchinovski, InfAI/DBpedia Association, TIB, CTU Prague

Abstract

The Knowledge Graph ecosystem is rapidly growing, but discovering, understanding, and reusing Knowledge Graphs remains challenging: Where are relevant Knowledge Graphs? What do they contain? Which versions are available, and where can the data be accessed?

This talk introduces the DBpedia Knowledge Graph Catalog, an initiative for discovering, describing, and reusing Knowledge Graphs. While the Linked Open Data Cloud focuses on datasets, links, and the broader ecosystem, the KG Catalog takes a complementary perspective centred on Knowledge Graphs, their metadata, releases, and artifacts. Versioning, access, and machine-readable metadata are treated as first-class elements, enabling not only discovery but also operational reuse.

The talk presents the motivation and concepts behind the KG Catalog, demonstrates how Knowledge Graphs can be described and accessed through the catalog, and provides a live demonstration using real-world examples. Finally, it introduces the broader vision of building a comprehensive Knowledge Graph landscape and invites the community to contribute by cataloging Knowledge Graphs, curating metadata, and building services on top of the catalog.

From SOPs to Agent-Ready Semantic Layers: Business Rules, Business Objects, and Data Grounding for Enterprise AI

Chair: Thomas Kaminski

Session details
From SOPs to Agent-Ready Semantic Layers: Business Rules, Business Objects, and Data Grounding for Enterprise AI

Enterprises have built extensive data infrastructure, but their data products, operational guidance, and AI agents often lack a shared business context. Concepts such as Customer, Account, or Policy are defined differently across systems. SOPs and policy documents refer to these concepts without explicit connections to the underlying data, while agents are developed with their own tools and hand-wired grounding. Connecting these assets through shared business meaning creates a foundation for reuse, traceability, and governed enterprise AI. This tutorial introduces the Enterprise Semantic Layer as a machine-readable abstraction spanning physical assets such as schemas, APIs, and services; logical mappings; and business definitions, processes, capabilities, and ownership. Its central argument is that Business Object ontologies provide a common point of connection for data, operational guidance, and agents. Data product schemas are represented in RDF and mapped to these ontologies, establishing how business concepts are represented across heterogeneous sources and where their data can be found. SOPs, policy documents, and individual rules are represented as entities in the same graph and linked to the Business Objects they reference. A rule containing a condition about a customer can therefore connect to a shared definition of Customer and, through the mappings, to the data products supplying the relevant information. The rule retains its authored wording while gaining a traceable path to the data needed to apply it. Cataloguing agents, the rules they handle, and the tools they use brings execution into this shared context. Recording what tools read, what they write, and which systems they act against makes dependencies visible across operational guidance, agents, and data. Links to business processes, capabilities, and functions extend this traceability to the enterprise activities they support. A live demonstration will show a Customer Business Object ontology mapped across four non-semantic data products, enabling unified discovery and federated queries across sources with no common schema. The tutorial will also explain how the same Business Objects connect procedural guidance and agents to this data foundation, and how open semantic standards keep these connections portable across systems and vendors. The session is intended for practitioners and researchers working on knowledge graphs, semantic governance, enterprise AI, ontology engineering, and multi-agent systems. Learning objectives By the end of the session, participants will understand how to: - Construct an Enterprise Semantic Layer across physical, logical, and business levels. - Represent data product schemas in RDF and map them to shared Business Object ontologies. - Enable semantic discovery and federated queries across heterogeneous data products. - Connect SOPs, policy documents, and individually referenceable rules to Business Objects while preserving their authored text. - Trace business concepts referenced in rules to the data products supplying the relevant information. - Catalogue agents, the rules they handle, and their tools, including inputs, outputs, and target systems. - Trace dependencies across rules, agents, data sources, and business capabilities to support governance and change impact analysis.

Abstract

From Sequential Language to Structural Intelligence: Toward Relationship-Aware AI

Alex Romanova

Abstract

Large language models have transformed artificial intelligence by showing how much can be learned from language at scale. Their success has made language appear to be the dominant substrate of intelligence. This Blue Sky paper argues that this view is incomplete. We focus on one specific gap: what may be missing when relationships and structure are learned primarily through language. We argue that LLMs face two language bottlenecks. First, they are token-sequence systems: even with attention and rich internal representations, they process and generate language through sequences of tokens. Second, their training data is human language, which is the sequential expression of cognition rather than the full relational process from which cognition emerges. Neuroscience gives reason to take this distinction seriously: the connectome and network neuroscience describe the brain in terms of elements, connections, and graph-like organization, while recent work argues that language is primarily a tool for communication rather than thought itself. This suggests that LLMs learn from linguistic traces of intelligence, but not necessarily from the full relational processes that produce those traces. We therefore argue for relationship-aware AI as an open research direction: not a solved architecture, but a search for ways to combine linguistic fluency with explicit awareness of relationships and structure. The central distinction is between inference-time use of relationships and training-time learning from relationships. Many current systems add structure after a language model has already been trained, through retrieval, graph augmentation, tools, memory, or verification layers. These approaches are important, but the deeper Blue Sky challenge is to make relationships part of representation learning itself, so that structural awareness is learned by the model rather than supplied only after training.

Blerina Spahiu and Loris Roveda

Abstract

Knowledge graphs (KGs) have become powerful instruments for representing, integrating, and querying knowledge. Humanoid robots expose a fundamental limitation of current world models: they can predict future states, but they rarely make explicit the semantic commitments needed to decide whether an object is reachable, a surface is supportive, a human is too close, or an action is unsafe. This paper argues that the next long-term challenge for Semantic Web and Knowledge Graph research is to build \emph{Embodied Knowledge Graphs}: dynamic, multimodal, uncertainty-aware semantic world models whose statements are grounded in geometry, perception, proprioception, affordances, physical constraints, and predicted action consequences. The proposal is intentionally high-risk: it asks whether knowledge graphs must become useful not only by representing the world, but by making embodied action safer, more recoverable, and more auditable than systems based only on latent world models or scene graphs.

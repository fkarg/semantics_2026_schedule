From Paraphrase to Provenance: Building a Verbatim Claim-Spine from Spoken-Word Archives

Chad Nantais, Syntopic

10:20 – 11:50 CEST · Kraakhuis

Session time; individual talk times are not specified in the timetable.

Chair: Milan Dojchinovski

Online room

Abstract

Most pipelines that turn unstructured text or audio into a knowledge graph quietly introduce a provenance gap. A language model is asked to extract "the claims," and it returns fluent paraphrases that no speaker ever actually said, often stamped with timestamps the model guessed rather than measured. The resulting graph reads well and cites nothing verifiable. This talk is a lessons-learned account of discovering that failure mode in a production pipeline and rearchitecting the extraction around a strict provenance contract.

The setting is Syntopic, a boutique service that transforms finished podcast back-catalogs into research-grade companion sites with synced transcripts, entity pages, and full-text search over a structured claim graph. Our worked example is an 81-episode philosophy archive (roughly 170 hours of audio) rendered into a navigable knowledge graph of claims, concepts, people, and works. Midway through the build we found our extraction was storing synthesized claim prose with LLM-estimated timestamps rather than verbatim spans anchored to deterministic, word-level speech-to-text output. That violated the core promise of the product, so we paused and separated every claim into a normalized headline plus one or more verbatim grounding spans, each carrying a derived timestamp from word-level Whisper output and an epistemic status tag (asserted, hedged, contested, or reported).

The talk covers the schema decisions this forced (stable entity IDs and canonical names kept separate from surface mentions, so the graph can survive re-processing and multi-client federation without migration), the human-in-the-loop correction log we capture as a compounding training asset, and the practical entity-resolution ceiling we hit as archives grow past a couple hundred episodes. The aim is to share concrete, reproducible tactics for anyone extracting grounded, citable claims from speech, where the difference between "sounds right" and "provably said" is the whole point.

A live companion site for a completed 81-episode archive will be available at presentation time.
